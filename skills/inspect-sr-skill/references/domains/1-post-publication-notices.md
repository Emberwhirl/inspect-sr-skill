# Domain 1: Inspecting post-publication notices

## What this domain asks

Search the journal record and Retraction Watch for retractions, expressions of concern, other notices, and the author team’s integrity track record. A retracted **main results paper** (not replaced) typically ends the whole assessment. Other Domain 1 hits contribute to the domain judgement; they do not automatically equal serious.

Repeat **1.1** and **1.2** shortly before finishing the review.

| Check | Question |
|-------|----------|
| **1.1** | Does the study have an associated retraction? |
| **1.2** | Does the study have an associated expression of concern or other relevant post-publication notice? |
| **1.3** | Do other studies by the research team highlight causes for concern? |

---

## 1.1 Does the study have an associated retraction?

**When to use**: Every report of the index study; repeat before lock.

**How**:
- Open the article on the **journal website** (notices may be unindexed) **and** search the **Retraction Watch database** (`retractiondatabase.org`) by DOI. The database has typographic errors; DOI is more reliable than title/author.
- Do **not** use the search box on `retractionwatch.com` (the blog). That is not a search of the database.
- A retraction means the journal no longer stands by the article. There should be a marked article plus a separate retraction notice with its own DOI. Journals are inconsistent; bibliographic databases index poorly.
- COPE describes retraction reasons; notice text may be thin and may not list all of the journal’s concerns.
- **Removed or withdrawn** articles (confidentiality, libel, copyright/IP) are treated the **same as retractions**.
- `Yes` on 1.1 **typically** warrants `serious concerns` for Domain 1 **and overall**, regardless of the stated reason, **particularly if it is the main results paper** — remaining INSPECT-SR checks need not continue.
- **Exception**: retracted and **replaced** (e.g. to correct an error). Assess the replacement with INSPECT-SR; do not inherit serious from the dead version.

**Avoid**: Blog search; ignoring withdrawals because they are not labelled “retraction”; treating a replacement version as still serious without assessing it.

**Worked example**: Gestational-diabetes probiotic trial; journal page replaced by a retraction notice after third-party integrity concerns and external statistical review; “the article’s conclusions are unreliable.” Answer `Yes` → Domain 1 and overall `serious concerns`. Stop.

Further search guidance: Cochrane Handbook Chapter 4 technical supplement. Consult an information specialist if needed.

## 1.2 Does the study have an associated expression of concern or other relevant post-publication notice?

**When to use**: Same search pass as 1.1; repeat before lock.

**How**:
- EoCs, notifications, publisher notes, editor notes are used **less consistently** than retractions. EoCs generally mean concerns exist but evidence is inconclusive or unresolved. Other notes may flag an issue or give a status update.
- **Read the content and purpose** of the notice. Text may under-represent the journal’s concerns.
- If the notice says **investigation ongoing**, revisit the journal website before finishing the review.
- Check journal site + Retraction Watch while doing 1.1.
- Also consider **letters to the editor** and **PubPeer** (and similar) **when they relate to trustworthiness**. Citation-search the trial in Web of Science or Scopus; search PubMed/Medline; PubPeer browser plugin flags comments while you read.
- Critical correspondence or PubPeer **must not automatically** trigger concerns: some critiques lack merit or do not concern trustworthiness. Use them as pointers to features you then verify in the matching domain.
- Unlike 1.1, a 1.2 answer **contributes to** the domain judgement; it does not by itself typically end the assessment.

**Avoid**: Equating any PubPeer comment with `Yes`; judging from the label “Editor’s Note” without reading it; skipping letters/PubPeer after a clean journal page; ignoring an ongoing investigation until after the review publishes.

**Worked examples**:
- DOI hit on retractiondatabase.org labelled “Concerns/Issues About data”; journal **Editor’s Note** that the article is under investigation for integrity → `Yes`.
- PubPeer attacking **dose** of a weight-loss drug; no journal notices; not about trustworthiness of data → `No`.

## 1.3 Do other studies by the research team highlight causes for concern?

**When to use**: Every assessment.

**How**:
- Search **first, corresponding, and last author** (minimum) on Retraction Watch. Repeat with given/family names **switched** (journals transpose names).
- A trustworthiness track record **may introduce doubts** about the index study.
- **Read the notices**. A prior retraction for an **honest error** may not warrant concern from track record.
- Middle-author searches are optional. If you do them, use the index paper’s **contribution statement** to judge whether that person’s integrity history is enough to transfer concern to this trial.
- Integrity comments on the team’s **other** papers (letters, PubPeer, not from the journal) may help you spot problems in the **index** study; they are not an automatic domain-level serious.

**Avoid**: Any retraction on any co-author → `Yes`; counting hits without reading notices; treating 1.3 `Yes` as equivalent to a retracted main paper.

**Worked examples**:
- Last-author search returns numerous retractions and EoCs about **data integrity**; reviewer reads a sample to confirm the theme → `Yes`.
- Similar **concurrent** trial by the same team retracted; notice alludes to lack of transparency but is unclear on motive → `Yes`.

---

## Reaching the domain judgement

1.1 on an unreplaced main results paper typically **is** domain + overall serious. 1.2 and 1.3 feed the domain rating without a Yes-count rule. Report reasons.
